Each footnote carried a short descriptive label I wrote so it would read at the back of a ballroom. On all but one slide that label is not what the paper is called. The titles below are the published titles, taken from the primary source and cross-checked against the publisher's registered metadata, and the label I put on the slide is shown underneath each one so you can match what you saw to what was published. If you searched for one of those labels and found nothing, that is why.

Two works below carry a dashed cited here, not on a slide chip. Each supports something I said from the podium whose citation never made it onto the slide. They are marked rather than blended in, so the page shows both what the talk cited and what it should have.

What is here, and what is not.

Every work cited in a footnote on a slide is here, together with the four also cited on the resources page, the two noted above, and the twenty-one references the demonstration produced. Nothing else is.

The resources page also carries figures the companies publish about themselves — how often Epic says its chart summarization is used, how many clinicians Microsoft reports for Dragon Copilot, which journals OpenEvidence says it has licensed. Those are vendor statements, they are labelled as vendor statements where they appear, and they are not references, so they are not here. Neither are the videos, essays and prompting guides on that page, which are worth your time and have no DOI. Papers I read while building the talk but did not draw on are not here either, because this page is a record of what the talk actually rested on.

Assembled by the agent, not by me

The twenty-one references that follow came out of the recorded demonstration rather than out of the talk. An agent was given one clinical question — fresh versus elective frozen single embryo transfer — and built its own reference list from it. I did not choose a single paper on it, and I have not removed one.

Every number the run asserted was then sent to two frontier models from two different vendors. Each was instructed to retrieve the source itself rather than trust an excerpt, and to try to refute the claim rather than confirm it. The tag on each reference is the arithmetic result of those two verdicts, applied in code rather than decided by a model. A paper can carry more than one tag because more than one claim was drawn from it.

verified x2
Both verifiers retrieved the source independently and confirmed the number.
disputed
The verifiers disagreed. The disagreement is quoted in full in the run's adjudication file rather than resolved silently.
refuted x2
Both verifiers rejected the claim. It was dropped from the run's conclusions.
unverified
Full text could not be obtained, so the claim was excluded.

A tag describes what happened to a claim during one run. It is not a verdict on the paper.

disputed against a Cochrane review means two models read one figure out of it differently on one afternoon. It does not mean the review is unsound, and nothing here should be read as my assessment of any of these studies. The run has not been reviewed for accuracy by a human expert. It is a demonstration of a method for checking work, published exactly as the run produced it, and it is not medical advice. The explainer it wrote and the full adjudication table carry every verdict and every disagreement.

The demonstration ran at slides 30 to 35. Its references are listed last rather than in that position, because twenty-one entries wedged into the middle would bury the six that follow.
