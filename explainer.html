Fresh versus elective frozen single embryo transfer

What the randomised evidence supports, what it does not, and where two independently retrieving language models disagreed about the numbers.

01

The bottom line

Elective cryopreservation of all embryos does not raise cumulative live birth in an unselected IVF or ICSI population, and the apparent per-transfer advantage of segmentation is largely an artefact of the denominator. In the Cochrane review of randomised trials the cumulative odds ratio is 1.08 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.95 to 1.22 and I² of 0% across eight trials and 4712 women, while the same review's analysis of live birth after the first transfer alone gives an odds ratio of 1.17 with a confidence interval of 1.06 to 1.28 and I² of 74% across thirteen trials and 7766 women. The review states in its own summary table that the first-transfer comparison is included for illustrative purposes, is biased against the fresh arm because a fresh transfer cancelled for ovarian hyperstimulation risk is counted as a failure while the corresponding frozen transfer is simply deferred, and that cumulative live birth is the outcome relevant to women.

The dissolution of the per-transfer effect is visible inside single trials rather than only across them, which is what makes it an accounting phenomenon rather than a population phenomenon. Chen and colleagues randomised 1508 women with polycystic ovary syndrome to segmentation or fresh cleavage-stage double embryo transfer and found live birth after the first transfer of 49.3% against 42.0%, a risk ratio of 1.17 with a confidence interval of 1.05 to 1.31 and an absolute difference of 7.3 percentage points, yet cumulative live birth in the same trial was 465 of 746 against 455 of 762 and was not significant. Wei and colleagues randomised 1650 ovulatory women with good prognosis to single blastocyst transfer and found singleton live birth of 50% against 40% after the first transfer, a risk ratio of 1.26 with a confidence interval of 1.14 to 1.41, while the cumulative comparison extracted by Cochrane for the same trial is an odds ratio of 1.16 with a confidence interval of 0.93 to 1.45. Two trials with different populations, different embryo stages and different transfer numbers converge on the same structure, in which segmentation moves the first success earlier without producing more children per oocyte retrieval.

The case for segmentation therefore rests on ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome, and there the randomised evidence is unambiguous in direction while being modest and strongly population-dependent in magnitude. Six trials pooled in Cochrane give an odds ratio of 0.26 with a confidence interval of 0.17 to 0.39 and I² of 0%, with 17 events in 2226 women assigned to freeze-all against 83 in 2252 assigned to fresh transfer, which is an absolute reduction of 2.92 percentage points and a number needed to treat of 34. That pooled figure conceals a range from 8.6 percentage points in predicted high responders with at least eighteen follicles of eleven millimetres or more, where the number needed to treat is 12, down to 0.6 percentage points in ovulatory women of good prognosis, where it is 165. The pooled estimate also excludes the single largest trial in the field, Shi and colleagues with 2157 ovulatory women, in which high-risk women were excluded by design.

Against that sits an obstetric cost that is carried by randomised data and not only by registries. Singleton birth weight is higher after frozen transfer by a mean of 127.44 grams with a confidence interval of 77.08 to 177.80 and I² of 0% across five trials, the only outcome in the review graded at moderate certainty, and large-for-gestational-age birth is more frequent with a Peto odds ratio of 1.96, confidence interval 1.51 to 2.55 and I² of 0%. Small-for-gestational-age birth runs in the opposite direction. The hypertensive signal is the one that should govern counselling and it is also the one on which the two verifiers used here disagreed, so it is presented below with the disagreement quoted rather than resolved.

Live birth

No cumulative advantage. The per-transfer advantage is a denominator effect and dissolves within individual trials.

OHSS and safety

A real and mechanistically coherent reduction, modest in absolute terms and concentrated in high responders, set against higher birth weight and more large-for-gestational-age infants.

Subgroups

No formal interaction test by ovulatory status exists anywhere. PGT-A trials freeze both arms and cannot speak to the question at all.

04

The evidence in detail

Live birth, and the two denominators

Keeping the two denominators apart is not a stylistic preference in this literature, it is the difference between two opposite conclusions drawn from the same trials, and the Cochrane review demonstrates both from one dataset. The cumulative analysis is tight and null with every trial-level diamond crossing unity and I² of 0%; the first-transfer analysis is heterogeneous at I² of 74% with the two heaviest contributors pointing in opposite directions, Shi and colleagues at an odds ratio of 0.94 carrying 32.8% of the weight against Wei and colleagues at 1.61 carrying 18.9%. Heterogeneity of that magnitude in the per-transfer analysis alongside its complete absence in the cumulative analysis is itself evidence that the per-transfer contrast is measuring something other than the capacity of a cohort of embryos to produce a child.

The contemporary single embryo transfer trials in unselected Western populations are null. Stormlund and colleagues randomised 460 women across Denmark, Sweden and Spain to single blastocyst transfer and found ongoing pregnancy per woman randomised of 27.8% against 29.6%, a risk difference of 1.8 percentage points against segmentation with a confidence interval of −10.5 to 7.0. The E-Freeze trial randomised 619 couples across eighteen United Kingdom clinics with more than four fifths receiving a single embryo and its point estimate favoured fresh transfer. The only pivotal trial that is both single embryo transfer and positive on live birth is Wei and colleagues, whose primary outcome is singleton live birth in a good-prognosis Chinese cohort requiring at least four day-three embryos, and whose cumulative result is null. The positive signal for freeze-all therefore rests on older double embryo transfer trials in polycystic ovary syndrome together with one single embryo transfer trial in a selected population, while trials that resemble current practice in unselected patients show no benefit.

One structural caveat governs how far any of this transfers to a single embryo transfer clinic. The cumulative Cochrane estimate is weighted 56% to a cleavage-stage double embryo transfer subgroup and 44% to a blastocyst subgroup, with no subgroup difference on formal testing. Chen, Shi and Vuong all transferred up to two cleavage-stage embryos. Applying their live-birth estimates to elective single blastocyst transfer is an extrapolation across embryo number, embryo stage and cryopreservation era at once, and the two trials that avoid that extrapolation, Wei and Stormlund, disagree with each other.

Hyperstimulation, and the obstetric trade

Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome is the outcome for which segmentation has a coherent mechanism and a consistent randomised effect. Removing the fresh transfer removes the pregnancy-derived human chorionic gonadotrophin that sustains VEGF-mediated capillary permeability beyond the window of the exogenous trigger, which is why segmentation abolishes late-onset disease specifically rather than reducing hyperstimulation uniformly. The trials cannot be read as a clean test of that mechanism in every case, and the confounding is not evenly distributed. Chen, Shi, Vuong, Wei and E-Freeze used human chorionic gonadotrophin triggers in both arms and are unconfounded. Stormlund used a gonadotrophin-releasing hormone agonist trigger in the freeze-all arm against human chorionic gonadotrophin in the fresh arm and additionally converted 24 of 230 women in the fresh arm to freezing for hyperstimulation risk under protocol, which removes precisely the women who generate events, and that trial contributes nothing interpretable to the hyperstimulation question. Santos-Ribeiro and colleagues triggered every patient with an agonist in both arms and still found nine events in 105 women assigned to fresh transfer against none in 104 assigned to segmentation, which establishes that agonist trigger alone does not protect a high responder who then receives a fresh transfer with human chorionic gonadotrophin luteal support.

The hypertensive disorder signal is where the two verifiers parted company and where the underlying evidence is thinner than its frequent citation implies. The pooled Peto odds ratio of 2.15 with a confidence interval of 1.42 to 3.25 and I² of 29% rests on 92 events across three trials, and the largest trial ever conducted in this field, Shi and colleagues with 2157 women, reported pre-eclampsia at a risk ratio of 1.36 with a confidence interval of 0.77 to 2.42 and is absent from that pool. Nordic registry data with within-sibship analysis show an adjusted odds ratio of 2.02 with a confidence interval of 1.72 to 2.39 for frozen transfer against natural conception while fresh transfer is flat against the same reference at 0.99 with a confidence interval of 0.89 to 1.09, and that dissociation within sibships is the strongest available argument that the association attaches to the frozen transfer itself rather than to the couples who receive one. Registry data cannot randomise and are labelled as such throughout this document. Stormlund used modified natural cycle preparation with the corpus luteum preserved and found pre-eclampsia at 7.0% against 7.3%, which is the observation most consistent with the hypothesis that the hypertensive excess belongs to programmed cycles lacking a corpus luteum rather than to cryopreservation, and it is the reason the pooled estimate should not be applied to natural-cycle frozen transfer without qualification.

Subgroups, and what has never been tested

The proposition that segmentation benefits women with polycystic ovary syndrome and not ovulatory women is the organising claim of this field, and it has never been tested as an interaction. No formal treatment-by-ovulatory-status interaction term or meta-regression exists in the Cochrane review, whose only prespecified subgroup analyses are by embryo stage and whose primary-outcome subgroup test returns χ² of 0.07 on one degree of freedom with a P value of 0.78; the review states that it does not answer the population question. Within that review Chen, enrolling only women with polycystic ovary syndrome, and Vuong, excluding them by Rotterdam criteria, sit adjacent in the same cleavage-stage subgroup with cumulative odds ratios of 1.12 and 1.06. The secondary analysis most often cited as establishing the difference fits two separate multivariable models, one in women with polycystic ovary syndrome and one in ovulatory women, and its interaction terms are treatment by ovarian response fitted within each cohort rather than a pooled treatment by syndrome-status term, so reading a population difference out of significance in one model and non-significance in the other is the difference-of- significance fallacy.

Two findings break the syndrome-based reading directly. Wei and colleagues studied ovulatory women with regular cycles and found a large per-transfer advantage, so ovulatory status cannot be the discriminating variable; the difference between that trial and Shi, also ovulatory and drawn from overlapping Chinese investigator groups, is single blastocyst transfer against double cleavage-stage transfer. Stormlund studied ovulatory women with single blastocyst transfer and was null, so the trials disagree within that stratum as well. The variable that predicts the direction of the result across these trials more reliably than ovulatory status is whether the outcome was counted per transfer or per woman randomised. What survives as a defensible subgroup statement is narrower and physiological rather than syndromic: the absolute hyperstimulation benefit of segmentation scales with ovarian response, which is why it is largest in the trial that selected on follicle count.

Preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy cannot arbitrate the fresh against frozen question because in the pivotal trials it does not vary the exposure. STAR required blastocysts of sufficient quality for biopsy and vitrification and enrolled women intending single embryo transfer after warming; Yan and colleagues cryopreserved all embryos and performed single frozen transfers in both groups. Freezing is a constant in both designs, and the Cochrane review annotates the relevant forest-plot row to that effect. The one randomised design that varies freezing while holding biopsy constant is Coates and colleagues, in which every patient underwent testing and randomisation occurred at trigger, and even there the freeze-all arm could draw on day-six euploid blastocysts unavailable to the fresh arm, so part of the difference reflects a larger eligible embryo cohort rather than endometrial receptivity, and the adjusted odds ratio of 2.1 crosses unity with a confidence interval of 0.95 to 4.68. The finding from this literature that bears on segmentation is Yan's, where cumulative live birth was lower with testing, 468 of 606 against 496 of 606, a risk ratio of 0.94 with a confidence interval of 0.89 to 1.00, because it is the same per-transfer against cumulative inversion seen in the freeze-all trials.

What is still uncertain

  • The individual participant data meta-analysis is not in this document, and it is the single most likely thing to change it.
    Both verifiers independently established that the INFORM collaboration has reported results, refuting the retrieval agent's claim that only a protocol exists. Those results appear in conference abstract and preprint form rather than as a peer-reviewed full paper, which places them outside the inclusion rules fixed before searching. No INFORM estimate has been run through the two-verifier pipeline and none appears in any table here. Both verifiers reported its first-transfer live-birth estimate as null, which if confirmed on peer-reviewed publication would bring the per-transfer analysis into line with the cumulative analysis and remove the central tension in this literature.
  • No trial has tested whether the effect of segmentation differs by ovulatory status.
    The PCOS against ovulatory contrast rests entirely on comparison between separate trials. No pooled interaction term or meta-regression on that axis exists in the Cochrane review, in the secondary analyses, or in any meta-analysis retrieved. Both trial groups say the reason for the discrepancy is uncertain.
  • The pivotal live-birth evidence is not single embryo transfer evidence.
    Chen, Shi and Vuong transferred up to two cleavage-stage embryos. The cumulative pooled estimate is weighted majority to that subgroup. The two single blastocyst transfer trials disagree with each other.
  • The largest trial in the field is missing from the pooled hyperstimulation and hypertensive analyses.
    Shi and colleagues, with 2157 women, is absent from the Cochrane hyperstimulation pool and from the hypertensive disorders pool, and it reported a null pre-eclampsia result. E-Freeze and Stormlund were published after the Cochrane search closed on 23 September 2020 and sit in no meta-analysis at all.
  • Whether the hypertensive excess belongs to freezing or to programmed endometrial preparation is unresolved.
    The pooled estimate derives from trials using programmed cycles. The one trial using modified natural cycle preparation found no difference. No randomised comparison of preparation regimens powered for hypertensive disorders exists.
  • One claim is excluded because a verifier could not obtain the source.
    The 2022 Obstetrics and Gynecology meta-analysis of comprehensive-chromosome-screening trials is closed access, and although one verifier reached it the other could not, so it is tagged unverified and appears in no table. Its advanced-maternal-age cutoff is therefore not asserted anywhere in this document, and whether its pooled estimate is per woman or per transfer remains unestablished.
  • Timing labels on the responder and high-responder analyses are contested.
    The two verifiers disagreed on whether several live-birth estimates in the responder-stratified meta-analysis and the high-responder trial are per transfer or per woman randomised under intention to treat. Those claims are retained with the disagreement quoted, and the outcome table does not restate the contested framing. One of those sources also carries internally inconsistent sample sizes, which both verifiers confirmed independently and which are flagged rather than propagated.
08

What would change practice

Segmentation should be selected for on the basis of hyperstimulation risk and not offered as a default strategy to improve live birth, because the live-birth premise does not survive cumulative accounting and the obstetric cost is real. In a predicted high responder, an agonist trigger with elective cryopreservation of all embryos is justified by an absolute hyperstimulation reduction of roughly eight percentage points with a number needed to treat near twelve, and that justification does not require any live-birth advantage. In an ovulatory woman of average response, the same strategy buys an absolute hyperstimulation reduction under one percentage point, requires more than one hundred and fifty women treated to prevent one case, and carries a higher probability of a large-for-gestational-age infant and a mean birth weight increment of about 127 grams. Both societies that have addressed the question in a formal document decline to endorse routine use, with ESHRE stating in its 2023 good practice recommendations on add-ons that elective freeze-all is currently not recommended for routine clinical use, and ASRM in its 2024 committee opinion recording that potential risks of a freeze-all strategy include increased risk of maternal hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and having a large-for-gestational-age infant. ASRM has no standalone practice document on elective freeze-all.

Three results would change this. The first is an adequately powered randomised trial restricted to single embryo transfer in an unselected population, reporting cumulative live birth per woman randomised across all embryos from one retrieval with a prespecified treatment-by-ovulatory-status interaction term, which would convert the central subgroup claim of this field from a between-trial comparison into a tested hypothesis. The second is an individual participant data meta-analysis with the trial-level data needed to fit treatment-covariate interactions across the whole trial set; the INFORM collaboration was designed for exactly this and is discussed under uncertainty below. The third is a randomised comparison of endometrial preparation, natural or modified natural against programmed, powered for hypertensive disorders of pregnancy rather than for pregnancy rate, because if the hypertensive excess is a corpus-luteum phenomenon then it is a property of how frozen transfers are prepared rather than of freezing, and the safety side of this decision would need rewriting rather than rebalancing.

Method note

Four retrieval agents worked one sub-question each, fetching primary full text or PDF and recording effect size, confidence interval, absolute numbers, design, denominator, DOI, PMID, an exact quoted sentence and a section or page for every claim. Key results held in forest plots were read visually from the rendered figure rather than from a text layer.

Every claim then went to two verifiers instructed to refute rather than to agree, with independent retrieval paths and an explicit prohibition on reading each other's cached artefacts. Agreement between two models about an excerpt handed to them tests only whether they can read; agreement after each separately retrieves the source is what the design is for. Verdicts were tallied in code: both confirmed keeps a claim as verified twice, one confirmed against one disputed keeps it only with the disagreement quoted inline, and either verifier failing retrieval excludes it from the bottom line and the summary table. The both-disputed case was not covered by the rule as specified and is handled as refuted twice, also excluded, and flagged here so the extension is not mistaken for the original rule.
