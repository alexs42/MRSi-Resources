The four rules, if you remember nothing else

  1. Match the tool to the risk, and the data to the tool.
  2. Ground every factual claim in a source, and verify the citation and the number before you reuse it.
  3. Keep PHI inside approved, BAA-covered or on-prem systems.
  4. Preserve skill through unaided practice and audit.

The red line — decide what never gets pasted

Consumer — never PHI Free or personal web apps carry no business-associate agreement, so de-identified questions and drafting only.
Enterprise no-train — still not cleared for PHI Paid endpoints that contractually do not train on inputs lower the leak risk, but without a signed BAA they are not cleared for identifiable data.
BAA-covered or on-prem — the only tier for identifiable data And only for the approved use.

The prompt box is a disclosure.

Under HIPAA, any vendor that handles ePHI needs a BAA even in “no-view” encrypted setups, and the “don’t train on my data” toggle stops training reuse — it does not create a BAA. IVF data resists de-identification: PGT genetics and partner linkage are inherently identifying, so the genome is the identifier.

1 · The tools you will meet in clinic

Most of what follows this section is developer tooling. This is the part that reaches a consulting room. It is an inventory of what exists and what is known about it, not advice on what to buy, and nothing here is endorsed by me or by my institution. Study findings carry a citation; adoption figures are labelled as the vendor's own. Availability was checked on 28 July 2026 and this field moves quickly.

Evidence at the point of care

  • OpenEvidenceFree to healthcare professionals and carries advertising; professional status may be verified · the company reports content agreements with NEJM, JAMA, NCCN, Wiley and Cochrane, and announced a collaboration with ACOG in May 2026
  • UpToDate Expert AIReached through an enterprise subscription or included in select personal ones, with availability varying by region · Wolters Kluwer states that answers are drawn only from UpToDate content and linked back to the topic

The vendors describe different source boundaries, and the difference is the whole point of choosing between them: OpenEvidence licenses primary journal content, so an answer can carry the trial itself, while UpToDate Expert AI is confined to text that a named editorial process has already reviewed. Neither description is evidence about the quality of any particular answer, and neither route is automatic — Expert AI arrives through an institutional agreement or a qualifying personal subscription, not with every UpToDate login.

Ask what funds the tool you are using. OpenEvidence states that it provides the service free to healthcare professionals and shows a limited number of advertisements from vetted advertisers, and that this is what allows the service to be free. Its privacy policy describes disclosing a clinician's National Provider Identifier and the timestamp of an advertisement interaction to confirm that a verified professional saw that advertisement. A separate programme, described as United States only, may share hashed email addresses, cookie and device identifiers, IP addresses and a professional identifier with advertising partners to deliver and measure advertising on other sites. The same policy states that user questions and conversations are not shared, that models are not trained on protected health information, and that processed data are secured in compliance with HIPAA.

No system earned a safety margin.

An independent comparison published this year put both tools against general-purpose models on real clinical questions, and the result is the part worth carrying out of the room. On 500 MedQA items the frontier models led, Gemini 3.1 Pro at 97.4% and GPT-5.2 at 94.2%, against 89.6% for OpenEvidence and 88.4% for UpToDate. On 100 real physician queries scored by 12 blinded clinicians, the two specialised tools and a free Google AI Overview formed a single tier with no significant differences between them, at 3.24, 3.17 and 3.27 out of 4. Safety outcomes did not differ across models: none produced more harmful content or more hallucinations than any other. UpToDate Expert AI declined to answer 19% of the questions, against 1% to 3% for most of the others, which is either the most honest behaviour in the study or the least useful depending on the question you brought it.

Vishwanath K, Alyakin A, Ghosh M, et al. General-purpose large language models outperform specialized clinical AI tools on medical benchmarks. Nat Med 2026. doi:10.1038/s41591-026-04431-5

Inside the EHR — what Epic ships

Epic groups its clinician-facing AI under the name Art, with Emmie facing patients in MyChart and Penny working the revenue cycle.

  • Art — the clinician-facing setChart summarization ("Art's Insights") and drafted replies to patient messages in the In Basket are generally available
  • AI Charting, or "Chart with Art"Epic's own ambient documentation · released to all Epic EHR customers during the week of 4 February 2026, with a nursing version in March
  • CuriosityFoundation models trained on the de-identified Cosmos record set · a research preview, not in clinical use

Epic now sits on both sides of the scribe question, which is the thing most likely to surprise a clinician who has been following the vendor market. Epic says AI Charting listens during the visit, drafts the note and queues orders from the conversation, and Epic also continues to carry third-party scribes through its Toolbox programme, so the ambient tool running in your clinic may be Epic's own or a vendor's product working inside Epic. Chart summarization is the one already operating at scale: Epic said on 4 February 2026 that Art's Insights was being used more than 16 million times a month, a figure the company reports rather than one an independent study measured. Curiosity is worth naming only so that it is not mistaken for something a clinician can use today, because the trade coverage reads as though it were.

Ambient scribes

Ambient documentation is the tool class most likely to arrive in your clinic next, and the one that captures the most, since the microphone is open for the length of the visit. That places it inside the red-line question at the top of this page.

Two studies frame what these drafts are worth, and neither evaluated any of the products listed below — both tested model-generated clinical text in a research setting, so read them as evidence about the technique rather than about a vendor. In a ten-physician blinded reader study, the best adapted model summaries were judged equivalent to physician-written ones 45% of the time and superior in a further 36%, the expert preferred in only 19%, and the same study found hallucinations in 5% of model samples against 12% of the human ones. A fine-tuned model drafted discharge summaries in about 83 seconds against 30 to 50 minutes by hand, with 94% rated acceptable for the record after minor verification. Around 6% still carried a minor factual inconsistency, that timing excludes the reading and the signature, and the responsibility for what is signed stays with the clinician who signs it.

9 · A system prompt worth stealing

The standing instruction I give a model before anything else. The lines that earn their place are the ones giving the model permission to say no: tell me if the request is illogical, wrong, or there is no data after tool calls, and if there is no answer or the resource is not available, say so, do not invent plausible answers.

A model with no licence to fail will produce something rather than nothing, and something rather than nothing is how a fabricated citation ends up in your manuscript.

10 · The demonstration

An agent answering a real clinical question — fresh versus elective frozen embryo transfer — under a method built so a clinician can check it rather than trust it. A plan committed before any searching. A named primary source behind every number. Two frontier models from two different vendors retrieving those sources separately and instructed to refute rather than agree. An arithmetic rule, applied in code, deciding what survives.
